What should a CRM capture when a new lead comes in?

A CRM record should help the next person make a useful move—not just prove that a form was submitted. The best lead requirements balance enough context for action with a simple, respectful first interaction.

Start with what helps you respond

It is tempting to make every field mandatory: company size, budget, timeline, role, location, product interest, and more. But a long form can push a promising person away before a conversation begins. Start with a small set that makes a timely, relevant reply possible, then collect deeper qualification details as the relationship develops.

For most teams, the first record needs a reliable way to reach the person, a clear indication of what they asked about, and enough context to route the inquiry to the right owner.

A practical lead record, field by field

1. Identity and contact preferences

Capture a name and at least one verified contact channel, such as an email address or phone number. Keep communication preference and consent separate from the contact detail itself. A phone number does not automatically mean a person agreed to receive calls or texts.

2. Source and original request

Record where the lead came from—such as a referral, campaign, website, or event—and preserve the original question or message. Source gives marketing and sales teams useful attribution; the original request keeps the first response grounded in what the person actually needs.

3. Need, fit, and timing

Use a few clear fields to capture the problem the person wants to solve, the service or product they are considering, and their expected timeline. Budget, organization size, and decision role may be important for your sales process, but make them optional until you know they are necessary for routing or qualification.

4. Ownership and next action

Every open lead should have a responsible owner, a current stage, and a next step. A simple status such as “new,” “contacted,” or “meeting scheduled” is much more useful when it is paired with an action and a due time. Without ownership, even a complete record can sit untouched.

5. A concise activity history

Log meaningful interactions: when the team responded, what the person asked next, and whether a meeting or follow-up was agreed. A shared history prevents people from repeating questions and lets another teammate pick up the conversation with context.

Make data quality part of the workflow

Good CRM requirements are not only a list of fields. Use consistent picklists for stages and sources, validate email and phone formats, and agree on what qualifies a lead as “ready.” Keep free-text notes focused on relevant business context and avoid collecting sensitive personal details that the team does not need.

Review the fields with the people who actually respond to leads. If a field is routinely skipped, interpreted differently by each person, or never used to make a decision, simplify it or remove it. The goal is dependable, useful information—not the largest possible database.

Design for a better handoff

A strong lead record answers five practical questions at a glance: Who is this person? What are they asking for? How did they find us? Who owns the next response? What should happen next? When your CRM can answer those questions, a human can follow up sooner and with more relevance.

That is the standard to aim for: collect only what is needed, make consent explicit, and turn every new inquiry into a clear next step.
